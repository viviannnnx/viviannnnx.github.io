A.2

Part A · Recover Homographies

Computing H

A homography maps a point p = (x, y) in one photo to p' = (x', y') in the next, with H₃₃ = 1 so there are 8 unknowns. Each correspondence gives two linear equations, so my computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts) stacks them into A h = b. I clicked 12 pairs per image pair, which makes the system overdetermined (24 equations, 8 unknowns), and solve it with least squares.

Two rows of A and b per correspondence 
[ x  y  1  0  0  0  -x·x'  -y·x' ]  [h1..h8]ᵀ = [ x' ]
[ 0  0  0  x  y  1  -x·y'  -y·y' ]                [ y' ]

h = argmin ‖A h − b‖²      H = [[h1 h2 h3], [h4 h5 h6], [h7 h8 1]]
computeH 
def computeH(im1_pts, im2_pts):
    A, b = build_system(im1_pts, im2_pts)      # 2n x 8 system
    h, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, b, rcond=None)  # least squares
    return np.append(h, 1.0).reshape(3, 3)

Photos are labelled left → centre → right, and each side photo is matched to its neighbour. I picked sharp, distinctive features (corners of windows, signs, edges) spread across the whole overlap, since clustered points make H unstable.

A.3

Part A · Warp the Images

Warping & Rectification

Both warpImageNearestNeighbor(im, H) and warpImageBilinear(im, H) use inverse warping. I push the four image corners through H to get the output bounding box, map every output pixel back through H⁻¹ to a source coordinate, and sample there. This leaves no holes in the output. Pixels whose source coordinate falls outside the photo get alpha = 0 (returned as a mask). Pixel (0, 0) is the centre of the top-left pixel.

Interpolation 
nearest:   out(x, y) = im[ round(ys), round(xs) ]

bilinear:  a = xs − ⌊xs⌋,  b = ys − ⌊ys⌋
           out = (1−a)(1−b)·I00 + a(1−b)·I10 + (1−a)b·I01 + a·b·I11

Rectification

To test the warp, I rectified photos of known rectangles. I click the four corners (top-left, top-right, bottom-right, bottom-left) and map them to an upright rectangle with the object's real aspect ratio, using computeH with the corners as im1_pts and the rectangle as im2_pts. Order: input with clicked corners, nearest neighbor, bilinear.

Rectification 1 — Window, 3:10

Rectification 2 — Laptop, 3:5

Rectification 3 — Hush Valorant, 5:2

All three come out front-on. In Rectification 1 the window mullions are straight, with vertical bars upright and horizontal bars level, so the grid of panes reads as a regular rectangle. The reflections in the glass are not on the window plane (they are the trees and buildings across the street), so they look compressed and smeared, and the bottom rows are softer because the warp stretched them the most. In Rectification 2 the sticker sheet is upright and rectangular, but the stickers have thickness and a glossy finish, so their edges and highlights don't straighten perfectly, and the ASUS logo appears sideways because of how the lid was oriented in the photo. In Rectification 3 the banner frame is square and the "VALORANT 26" lettering is level and readable, though the fine small text is soft because the original photo was taken at a steep angle and the warp had to enlarge it.

Speed vs. quality

Quality: nearest neighbor copies the closest pixel, so edges and text look jagged and blocky, especially where the warp stretches the image. Bilinear averages the four surrounding pixels, so edges are smooth, at the cost of slight softness. Speed: nearest does one lookup per pixel, bilinear does four lookups and a weighted sum, so it is about 2.5–3.2× slower in my runs (timings above, from results/log.txt). Both finish quickly at this image size, so I use bilinear for the mosaics.

A.4

Part A · Blend into a Mosaic

Mosaics

The centre photo stays unwarped, and the left and right photos are warped into its frame. For a chain, I multiply homographies (the left photo's H into the reference is the product of its pair homographies; the right photo uses the inverse). I compute one bounding box that covers all warped photos, warp each onto that shared canvas with bilinear interpolation, and blend.

Weights. Each warped photo has a mask of valid pixels. Its weight is the distance transform of that mask, so the weight is 0 at the photo's border and grows toward its interior. Feathering averages the photos using these weights. Two-band blending splits each photo into a low-frequency part (Gaussian blur, σ = 8) and a high-frequency part (photo minus blur). The low frequencies are feathered for smooth colour transitions, and the high frequencies come from the photo with the largest weight at each pixel, which keeps detail sharp and avoids ghosting.

Blending 
feather = Σ wᵢ·Iᵢ / Σ wᵢ
two_band = Σ wᵢ·LP(Iᵢ) / Σ wᵢ  +  HP( I at argmaxᵢ wᵢ )     (HP = I − LP)

Distant, flat surfaces line up well in all three mosaics. In Mosaic 1 the building facade, columns and "Chan Shun Auditorium" lettering are continuous across the seams, and the two-band result shows no visible seam. The errors come from things that move or sit off a single plane. In Mosaic 1 a pedestrian who walked between shots leaves a faint ghost near the bottom right of the feathered result, and the people at the bottom left have shifted between photos. In Mosaic 2 the mirrored wardrobe doors show reflections that do not follow the homography, so the fridge appears twice (once for real and once in the mirror). The door frame also bends slightly at the top in the feathered result, which comes from nearby objects not lying on the wall plane. In Mosaic 3 the exposure differs between the three photos (the middle one is brighter than the right), and the glossy floor reflections, which change with viewpoint, blur a little in the overlap. All three canvases have large black corners and a bow-tie shape because the side photos are stretched most when warped into the centre frame.

Blending comparison

The same warped photos combined three ways: overwrite, feathering, and two-band.

Takeaway

Overwrite leaves hard seams and visible exposure jumps. The tree in Mosaic 1 is cut off at a vertical edge, the door in Mosaic 2 is duplicated, and the sofa in Mosaic 3 changes brightness halfway across. Feathering removes the seams, but fine detail ghosts or blurs where the photos are slightly misaligned, such as the pedestrian in Mosaic 1 and the door frame in Mosaic 2. Two-band keeps colour transitions smooth and detail sharp, and gives the cleanest result in all three mosaics, so I use it for the finals.
